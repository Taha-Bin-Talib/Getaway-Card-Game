Bhabhi Thulla

A fast, tense shedding game for 3 to 8 players. Get rid of every card in your hand and you are safe. Be the last one holding cards and you are the Bhabhi.

What is Bhabhi Thulla?

Bhabhi Thulla is a card game widely played across South Asia, usually among family and friends gathered around a table or a bed. It belongs to the family of shedding games: nobody collects points and nobody wins a pot. The only goal is to empty your hand. Players who run out of cards leave the game one by one, and the unlucky player left holding cards at the end takes the title of Bhabhi, a nickname that the table is usually happy to tease them about for the rest of the evening.

What makes the game special is the Thulla, a moment of pure drama. When you cannot follow the suit that was led, you may throw any card you like, and the player who was winning the trick has to pick up every card on the table. A single Thulla can swing the whole game, and a player who looked safe a minute ago suddenly holds a fistful of new cards.

Setup and the deal

Use one standard 52-card deck. Aces are high, so the order from strongest to weakest is A, K, Q, J, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2. Suits have no rank against each other; only the suit being led matters in each trick. The whole deck is dealt out round the table. With a number of players that does not divide 52 evenly, some players simply hold one card more than others.

The player holding the Ace of Spades opens the game by playing that card. Play then continues clockwise.

How a trick works

  1. The first card played in a trick is the lead, and its suit is the suit everyone else must follow.
  2. In turn, each player must play a card of the led suit if they have one.
  3. If everyone follows suit, the trick is complete. The player who played the highest card of the led suit wins it, all the cards go to the discard pile for good, and the winner leads the next trick.
  4. Players whose hands are empty after a trick has been resolved are out of the game and safe.

Notice what winning a trick means here: you do not collect anything. Winning only gives you the lead, which is often a burden, because you must put down a card first and you cannot choose the suit that suits the table. In Thulla, the best result is frequently to play a card that does not win.

The Thulla: when you cannot follow suit

If you hold no card of the led suit, you may play any card from your hand. That play is a Thulla. The trick stops immediately, even if other players have not yet played. The player who has so far played the highest card of the led suit must pick up every card on the table and add them to their hand. They then lead the next trick.

A Thulla has two effects at once. It is a gift to you, because you have shed a card you did not want, and it is a punishment for the unlucky player, who now holds more cards and has moved further from safety. The best Thulla throws are your most dangerous cards, such as high cards in suits where you hold few cards, because those are the cards most likely to hurt you later.

First-round protection

The opening trick, started by the Ace of Spades, can never be a Thulla. Everyone with a spade must follow with a spade. A player with no spades may throw any card lower than 10, and a player who holds no spade and no card below 10 simply passes. When the trick is finished, all its cards are discarded. This keeps the very first move fair, so that nobody is crushed before the game has begun.

Hand requests

Online, the host can enable an optional rule called the hand request. On your turn, before you play a card (and never in the opening trick), you may ask the next player for their entire hand, but only when they hold fewer than five cards. The asked player may agree or refuse. If they agree, depending on the room setting, they either hand over all their cards and leave the game, or you simply get to see their hand privately. Think carefully before accepting a request: handing over your cards eliminates you, which is a perfectly good way to become safe, but only if the opponent is more worried than you are.

The final duel

When only two players remain, the game changes. Each player's last card decides the outcome. If a player plays a last card that is smaller than their opponent's card, they lose the trick, they are safe, and the game ends with the other player as the Bhabhi. But if a player's last card is bigger, they win the trick and are not safe: they must blindly pick one card from the opponent's hand and play it. This repeats until someone's last card loses, or a pick empties a hand. The duel is short, tense and often decided by a single card.

Strategy tips

Lead low from long suits

When you must lead, play a low card from a suit in which you hold many cards. Low cards are unlikely to win the trick, and a long suit gives you plenty of safe cards for later. Avoid leading a suit in which another player has already shown a void, because that player will happily throw a Thulla onto you.

Duck as high as is safe

When you can follow suit, play the highest card that is still lower than the current winning card. You shed a strong card without taking any risk, and you save your small cards for situations in which you may need to go under.

Dump when you are last

If you are the last player in a trick and you can follow suit, nobody can throw a Thulla after you. This is the moment to get rid of your highest card of that suit, which is often a card you could never safely play at any other time.

Watch the voids

Pay attention to who could not follow suit. A player who has shown a void in hearts is a danger whenever you lead or win a trick in hearts. Keeping a mental note of the cards that have been discarded helps you recognize when your high card has become the highest remaining card of its suit, which makes it both powerful and dangerous.

Become void deliberately

Emptying a short suit early is useful, because from then on any trick in that suit gives you the chance to throw a Thulla. Players who are void in several suits are the ones who punish the table.

Remember the timer. In online rooms you have one minute per move. If it runs out, the game plays your highest legal card for you, which is rarely the best play.

Background

Like many family card games, Bhabhi Thulla has no single documented inventor, and the exact rules differ from region to region and even from household to household. Some groups allow different first-round rules, some play with several decks for large groups, and some treat the Thulla differently. The word Bhabhi means a brother's wife in several South Asian languages, and the game's tradition of giving the losing player that title is part of what makes it a social game as much as a competitive one. Frequently asked questions

How many people can play Bhabhi Thulla?

Between 3 and 8 players. Three or four players give the fastest games; larger groups mean more Thullas and more chaos.

Can the first round be a Thulla?

No. The opening trick is protected. Players without a spade throw a card lower than 10, or pass if they have none, and the whole trick is discarded.

What happens if two players empty their hands at the same time?

If every remaining player gets out together, the game is a draw. Otherwise the last player holding cards is the Bhabhi.

Do I have to play the highest card?

No. You only need to follow suit. Choosing which card of the suit to play is the main skill of the game.
